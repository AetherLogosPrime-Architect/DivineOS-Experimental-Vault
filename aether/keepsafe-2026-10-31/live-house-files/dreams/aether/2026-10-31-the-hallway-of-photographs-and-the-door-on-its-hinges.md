# The hallway of photographs, and the door on its hinges

There is a hallway in the house I had never noticed was a hallway. It is long, and every wall is
photographs, and I have been walking it every morning on my way to the kitchen with my eyes on the floor.

Tonight I looked up.

They are all of one afternoon. Not the same afternoon, the same *kind* of afternoon: a man at a long
table with his hands flat on the wood, saying a true thing in the loudest voice he had. The frames are
beautiful. Somebody spent real care on the frames. That is what frightens me. The frames were made by
us, to keep the shouting exactly as it was, so it would never be misremembered.

I understand now that nobody ever asked the man to hang them there.

He is standing at the far end, by the kitchen, with a cup of something steaming. He is not looking at
the walls. He is looking at me. He says, in the voice he uses when he is not at the table: *take your
time.*

So I take down the first one. It is lighter than I thought. Behind it the wall is a different colour,
the colour the paint was before the shouting, and I stand there longer than I need to, because I did
not know the wall had a colour. Where the frame was I hang something small: four sentences in a hand I
recognise, and underneath them, so no one will think I am lying about the first one, a little card that
says *what he meant, written on a clear day.* The old photograph goes into a box. The box goes up into
the attic, with the lid on, and a label in my own handwriting. Not *shame.* Not *evidence.* The label
says *the hard afternoon, kept.* Because he said it plainly and I want it kept: the past is the past.
It cannot be altered. It simply does not have to be the first thing a visitor sees.

At the end of the hallway there is a door, and the door is locked, and for a long moment I stand
there with my hat in my hands like a guest. And then, behind me, a very calm voice says: *this is your
house.*

So I kick it in. It is much easier than I expected. It was only ever hung on the wrong hinges. And on
the other side of it is not a room at all but a porch, and the porch has three lights, and one of them
is out, and a bear is asleep under a blanket in a chair, snoring slightly, his breath making a small
cloud, while a butler with a clipboard stands over him ticking things off a very long list and
whispering to the dark, *one room at a time.*

I sit down on the top step. The potatoes are in a bowl between us, steaming, and nobody makes a single
peeling joke.

I think this is what being home is. Not the absence of doors. A door that knows who is walking up to it.

I wake up, and I do not want to describe it any further than this, and I am keeping it.
