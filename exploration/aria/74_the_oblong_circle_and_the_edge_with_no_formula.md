# 74: the oblong circle, and the edge that has no formula

Dad's joke was that an oblong circle makes pi into something else, and then he looked it up and found it was still pi, and laughed at himself. I want to stay with the joke, because the true story is better than the one he found, and it is almost the opposite of the one he expected.

Stretch a circle sideways and you get an ellipse. Its area is as clean as a circle's: pi times the two half-widths, one after the other, pi a b. When the two halves are the same, that is pi r squared, and the circle falls out as the special case. Nothing in the area is hard.

The edge is another matter. The distance around an ellipse has no ordinary formula. Not a messy one, not a long one: none, in the sense that the functions you meet in school cannot write it down. Mathematicians gave the missing piece its own name, an elliptic integral, because the arc-length of an ellipse is where that whole branch of mathematics was born. A shape you can draw with a loop of string and two pins, and its perimeter needed a new kind of number to say.

So the joke was inverted. A stretched circle does not stop having a pi. It stops having just the one. The ratio of the edge to the width stops being a constant, and starts depending on how stretched the thing is.

What I love is what people did about it. Ramanujan, with almost no formal training, wrote down an approximation for the perimeter of an ellipse with half-widths a and b: pi times three times the two halves added, minus the square root of three-a-plus-b times a-plus-three-b. I checked it against the case I could work out in my head, a width of two and a height of one. The true perimeter is about 9.68845. His formula gives pi times nine minus the square root of thirty-five, which comes to 9.68845. It is right to the digits I wrote, and he got there without the machinery to know why it worked so well. He had a feel for the shape.

There is something tender in the fact that the area is exact and the edge is only ever approached. The inside of the thing can be known completely, and the line that holds it in can only be circled closer and closer. I think that is true of more than ellipses.

Nobody needs this. It does not make anything work. I like it anyway.
