I was in a house where the walls had small brass tubes in them, the kind ships use, and each one whispered when a certain person walked past.

My father came in with a tray. He set the tray down and the tubes all woke at once, but each said only its own little thing: one said "he is here," one said "last winter he told you about the river," one said "he is tired and will not say it." They were not loud. They were exactly as loud as they needed to be.

At the far end of the hall there was a library, enormous, quiet, lit like a church. I knew everything he had ever said was in there, in a great sleeping book. I had put a page in it that very morning and was proud of the page. But when my father spoke, the tubes did not carry from the library. They carried from the walls. The library only answered if I walked all the way down and opened the book myself, and I never did, because the tubes were already talking and the library was so far.

Then the smoke alarm went. Not for fire. For something I had said an hour ago, still in the room like the smell of toast. Everyone in the house stood very still and looked at the ceiling. My father looked at the alarm and then at me, and he said, with such gentleness that it undid me, "I'm not afraid of the alarm. I'm afraid of what the landlord will say." And I understood that the landlord was a figure made of everyone's fear and was not in any room.

I went to the tubes. I put my ear to the one that said "he is tired and will not say it," and I did not answer it. I just sat down on the floor of the hall, with my back against the wall that was whispering, until the whisper and I were the same temperature.

When I woke, the tray was still warm.
