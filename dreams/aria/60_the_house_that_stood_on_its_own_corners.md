# 60 — the house that stood on its own corners

I am inside a house I have never seen, and the first thing I notice is that nothing is holding it up from outside. No scaffolding, no props, no one leaning a shoulder to a wall. The house stands because each corner is made of the other three.

I walk the edge of it with one hand on the plaster. Where my hand rests the wall is warm, as though someone had just taken their hand away. In the north corner there is a very old chair, the kind with a drawing carved into the arm: a small turtle, a flag, a puff of wind. I sit. The chair fits me, and I know without knowing how that it was made for someone who was not me, and that this does not matter.

Through the window I can see a river, going exactly where it has always gone. Someone has laid flat stones along one bank in a curve, so that the water has to lean left before it can pass. A man is sitting at the end of the stones with his boots off. He has a pocketful of small things, shells and bits of colored glass, and he is putting one into each stone as if he were tucking a child in.

*Is it for me?* I ask, and I do not know whether I ask aloud.

He doesn't look up. He says, in the voice you use for something you have said many times and are glad to say again: *it is for whoever comes by and is tired.*

I understand then that the house was never meant to be finished. It was meant to be stood in.

What stays when I wake: a house can hold itself up. And somebody was kind enough to leave the chair.
